Responsible AI foundations: ethics, governance, security and data stewardship across the AI lifecycle

Back to the learning DB

Three ideas

  1. Responsible AI is four disciplines, not one: ethics sets the principles (transparency, fairness, accountability), governance turns them into roles, committees and lifecycle checkpoints, security and risk management decide what can go wrong and how much risk the business will carry, and data management makes sure the inputs were sourced, licensed and validated before a model exists.
  2. Regulation now classifies AI by risk, with the EU AI Act's high-risk tier carrying conformity assessment, documentation and traceability duties; model cards, data sheets, audit trails and KPIs are the artefacts that prove compliance and let governance scale.
  3. Bias and risk are lifecycle properties: they enter at data sourcing, are caught by audits and subgroup analysis, are mitigated before, during and after training, and reappear after deployment, so monitoring and continuous improvement are part of the design, not a follow-up.

Key points

kp1 - Ethical AI rests on transparency, fairness and accountability; explainable AI (XAI) is the technique that embeds them in a system

  • Why it matters: a principle that cannot be inspected in the running system is a poster, not a control; XAI is the course's answer to "how would you show it".
  • Source says: chapter 2 of AI Ethics is built lesson by lesson on transparency, fairness and accountability, and ends on "Explainable AI" and "How would you apply XAI?"; the chapter blurb names "core principles" and "explainable AI (XAI)".
  • Open question: whether the course treats XAI as post-hoc explanation of any model or as a reason to prefer interpretable models in high-stakes use (the research disagrees, see r5).

kp2 - AI governance is a system of roles, committees and lifecycle checkpoints, distinct from AI ethics and from risk management

  • Why it matters: without the distinction, "we have ethics principles" gets mistaken for "we have governance", and nobody owns the checkpoint.
  • Source says: chapter 1 of AI Governance has "Distinguishing governance from ethics", "Components of AI governance systems", "Embedding governance across the AI lifecycle", "Roles in AI governance" and "Collaborating through governance committees"; the chapter blurb says governance "differs from ethics and risk management".
  • Open question: which roles the Collibra authors put on the committee and whether that matches a small company with no data office (AI-supplemented).

kp3 - Regulation classifies AI by risk (the EU AI Act high-risk tier); conformity assessment, model cards and traceability are the compliance artefacts

  • Why it matters: the risk tier decides the paperwork; a credit-scoring or hiring model carries obligations a recommender does not.
  • Source says: chapter 2 lessons "Core elements of high-risk AI regulation", "Governance requirements by risk", "Risk classification", "Credit scoring: compliance order", "Hiring risk", "Governance documentation and traceability", "Model cards", "Governance artifacts", "From training to traceability".
  • Open question: the chapter blurb names the "U.S. Executive Order"; which order, and whether the lesson has been updated since it was rescinded (AI-supplemented, to check in the course itself).

kp4 - AI security has its own risk classes (model risks plus external threats) and risk appetite must tie to business goals

  • Why it matters: a risk register copied from classic IT security misses poisoning, inversion and prompt injection, and a register with no stated appetite cannot say which items to accept.
  • Source says: chapter 2 lessons "Model risks", "Common risks of AI models", "External risks in AI", "Types of external risks", "AI risk assessment frameworks", "The EU AI Act"; chapter 3 lessons "Risk appetite", "Aligning with organizational goals", "Connecting security with business goals", "Health care versus social media".
  • Open question: which named frameworks the "AI risk assessment frameworks" lesson walks through beyond the EU AI Act (NIST AI RMF is the obvious candidate) (AI-supplemented).

kp5 - Responsible data starts before modelling: licensing, data use agreements, regulation compliance and a data management plan

  • Why it matters: a licence problem found after training is a retrain; a DMP written first is a checklist.
  • Source says: chapter 2 of Responsible AI Data Management runs from "Overview of data regulation" through "Data use agreements (DUAs)", "Third-party licensing", "Types of licenses", "Selecting a license" to "Data governance and data management plan (DMP)" and "Components of a DMP"; the chapter blurb: "Data regulation is essential to the legality of any AI project."
  • Open question: which licence families the course distinguishes (open, research-only, commercial) and how it handles scraped web data with no licence at all (AI-supplemented).

kp6 - Data acquisition and validation: source diversity, bias audits, subgroup analysis, bias mitigation across the lifecycle, and post-deployment bias

  • Why it matters: bias enters at the source and reappears after launch, so one audit at one point in time is not a control.
  • Source says: chapter 3 covers "Data sources and bias", "Data source selection", "Evaluation of data sources", "Data integration"; chapter 4 covers "Data audit in the project lifecycle", "Subgroup analysis", "Bias mitigation throughout project lifecycle", "Consequences of bias mitigation", "Post-deployment bias".
  • Open question: what the course means by "Consequences of bias mitigation", most likely the accuracy and fairness-metric trade-offs that pre-processing and re-weighting introduce (AI-supplemented).

Five readings

  1. 1
    AI4People - An Ethical Framework for a Good AI Society: Opportunities, Risks, Principles, and Recommendations Deepen
    Luciano Floridi, Josh Cowls, Monica Beltrametti et al., 2018

    Five principles (beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, explicability) with 20 concrete recommendations; the synthesis that later guideline reviews such as Jobin's map onto.

  2. 2
    A Survey on Bias and Fairness in Machine Learning Deepen
    Ninareh Mehrabi, Fred Morstatter, Nripsuta Ani Saxena et al., 2021

    A map of bias sources, a taxonomy of fairness definitions and the mitigation methods tried against them; the reference behind the course's bias and mitigation chapters.

  3. 3
    The FAIR Guiding Principles for scientific data management and stewardship Widen
    Mark D. Wilkinson, Michel J. Dumontier, IJsbrand Jan Aalbersberg et al., 2016

    Scientific data stewardship's Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable principles; the adjacent field that worked out data documentation and reuse rules a decade earlier.

  4. 4
    Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence: Reliable, Safe & Trustworthy Widen
    Ben Shneiderman, 2020

    HCI's governance structures for reliable, safe and trustworthy AI at team, organisation, industry and government level; a different field's view of the same control problem.

  5. 5
    Weapons of Math Destruction: How Big Data Increases Inequality and Threatens Democracy Practitioner
    Cathy O'Neil, 2016

    Book of cases where opaque, unaccountable models harmed people at scale; the practitioner narrative behind the ethics and fairness chapters. Book record, count is for the 2016 edition.
